    1 · What "agentic AI" means

    An agent is a program that keeps doing four things in a loop, on its own, to reach a goal:

    PerceiveRead the world through tools: detectors, cameras, messages, data from connected cars.
    ReasonCompare options against its goals (less delay, fairness, safety) using rules, a learned policy or a language model.
    ActChange something in the world: keep the green, or switch to the other stage.
    Remember & talkKeep a memory of what happened and send messages to other agents.

    What makes it agentic rather than just "an algorithm" is the combination: it chooses which tools to use, keeps a goal over time, explains itself, works with other agents, and runs with a degree of autonomy. Because it acts in the real world, it always sits behind a safety shield: hard rules it cannot break.

    Environment
    Everything the agent can sense and affect: here, one junction and the traffic on it.
    Tool
    A function the agent can call, such as queue("NS") or priority_requests().
    Goal
    What "good" means, written as weights: e.g. 1 point per queued car, 0.15 points per second of the longest wait.
    Policy
    The rule that turns what it sees into an action. It can be hand-written, learned (reinforcement learning) or produced by a language model.
    Shield
    Hard safety rules checked after the agent decides and before the signal changes: minimum green, maximum green, intergreen, a fairness cap.

    2 · How an adaptive traffic signal works

    A signal shares one piece of road between conflicting streams by giving each stage a turn. One round of all stages is a cycle; how the cycle is shared is the split; when a junction's cycle starts relative to its neighbour is the offset. Between greens comes the intergreen (yellow + all-red), which is lost time.

    Fixed time
    A stored plan, the same every cycle. Cheap and predictable, but blind to today's traffic.
    Actuated
    Detectors at the stop line extend the green while cars keep arriving and end it after a gap.
    Adaptive
    A system measures demand and re-optimises cycle, split and offset continuously, e.g. SCOOT (model-based, upstream detectors) and SCATS (degree of saturation at the stop line).
    Max-pressure
    A simple decentralised rule: serve the stage whose waiting vehicles minus vehicles on its exit is largest.
    Agentic
    Adaptive control framed as an agent: tools, goals, memory, communication, explanations and a shield. It can use any of the above as tools.

    We judge controllers by delay (extra time compared with free flow), stops, queue length, the longest wait (fairness) and emissions.

    3 · Linkage 1: efficiency

    Efficiency means moving the most people with the least delay. An agent gets there by measuring instead of guessing: queues, arrivals and how full the exits are. One powerful signal is pressure: cars waiting to enter minus cars already on the exit. Serving the highest pressure keeps the network flowing and, importantly, avoids pushing cars into a road that is already full (spill-back).

    4 · Linkage 2: coordination

    Junctions close together should not act alone. If each upstream green is timed so that its platoon reaches the next junction on green, traffic rides a green wave. Classic systems set offsets centrally. Agents can instead talk: the upstream agent tells its neighbour "about 8 cars will reach you in 15 s", and the neighbour holds or starts its green for them.     5 · Linkage 3: eco-driving

    Every stop costs fuel: braking wastes energy and accelerating from rest burns more. If the signal tells approaching cars when it will turn green, a connected car can slow down early and glide through instead of stopping. This is GLOSA, Green Light Optimal Speed Advisory, using SPaT (Signal Phase and Timing) messages. There is a catch for adaptive control: an agent that keeps changing its mind cannot promise a time.     7 · Linkage 5: autonomous and connected vehicles

    A connected vehicle (CV) exchanges data with the infrastructure and other vehicles. An automated vehicle (AV) drives itself to some level (SAE levels 0 to 5). A CAV is both. For signal agents CAVs matter in three ways:

    • New eyes: CAVs report position and speed, so the agent can estimate queues without loops, if enough vehicles are connected.
    • New hands: CAVs can follow speed advice (eco-driving) or even platoon through on green.
    • New responsibility: mixed traffic (humans + CAVs) for decades means the agent must work at every penetration level.

    8 · Guardrails and levels of autonomy

    Traffic signals are safety-critical. A sensible design gives the agent freedom only inside a box the engineer can certify:

    LevelWho decidesExample
    0 · ManualEngineerFixed-time plans designed offline
    1 · AdvisoryAgent suggests, operator approvesAgent proposes new splits for tomorrow
    2 · SupervisedAgent acts within limits, operator watchesAgent chooses keep/switch; shield + centre oversight
    3 · CollaborativeAgents coordinate with each otherCorridor agents share platoon forecasts
    4 · AutonomousAgents set their own sub-goalsNetwork agents re-plan during an incident, logged for audit
    The shield is never optional: minimum green, maximum green, intergreen and pedestrian times are checked outside the agent, whatever kind of AI is inside.

    9 · Recap and memory route

    1. An agent loops perceive → reason → act, with tools, goals, memory and messages.
    2. Adaptive signals change cycle, split and offset; we score them by delay, stops, longest wait and emissions.
    3. Efficiency: measure queues and pressure; avoid spill-back.
    4. Coordination: neighbours share forecasts to make green waves.
    5. Eco-driving: SPaT + GLOSA turn stops into glides, if the signal is predictable.
    6. ITS: sensing, V2X, edge controller, agent, control centre.
    7. CAVs: new eyes, new hands, mixed traffic for a long time.
    8. Shield: hard rules outside the agent; autonomy is granted in levels.
